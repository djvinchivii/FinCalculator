## In plain English

When your money earns interest, that interest gets added to your balance. Next time, you earn interest on the bigger balance — so you earn **interest on your interest**. That's compound interest, and it's why money left alone for a long time can grow surprisingly large.

## When to use it

- Seeing how much regular saving (like $100 a month) could add up to
- Comparing "start now" against "start in five years"
- Understanding why time matters more than almost anything else

## Example

Put **$10,000** in an account earning **5%** a year (added monthly) and leave it for **10 years**. You'd end up with **$16,470.09** — that's $6,470.09 you never had to add yourself.

Or start with nothing and save **$500 a month** at **7%** for **30 years**. You'd put in $180,000, but end with about **$609,985**. More than two-thirds of that is growth.

## Watch out for

**Waiting to start.** Saving $500 a month from age 25 to 65 at 7% grows to about $1.3 million. Start at 35 instead and you end up with about $610,000 — less than half, even though you only skipped 10 years.

Also remember: investment returns aren't guaranteed. Some years go up, some go down. The rate you enter is a long-term average.

<details>
<summary>The math (optional)</summary>

For one deposit: **A = P × (1 + r/n)^(n × t)**

- **P** — what you start with
- **r** — the yearly rate as a decimal (7% → 0.07)
- **n** — how many times a year interest is added
- **t** — years

With regular deposits, the calculator works through every deposit one at a time using the rate per deposit period: `(1 + r/n)^(n/p) − 1`, where _p_ is how often you deposit.
